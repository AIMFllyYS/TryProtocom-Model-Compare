## 1. Block-world rule

Preserve voxel grammar. Terrain, trees, cliffs, architecture, props, and characters should read as constructed from cubes/cuboids or block-compatible forms. Avoid replacing important silhouettes with smooth photoreal geometry that breaks the world language.

Use smooth effects only where they support the world: fog, volumetrics, bloom, depth of field, snow, smoke, sparks, water effects, lens response.

## 2. Texture strategy

Prefer low-resolution pixel textures plus high-quality lighting.

Good combination:

- 16x16, 32x32, or 64x64 pixel-art textures;
- controlled roughness/specular response;
- ambient occlusion;
- soft or stylized shadows;
- global illumination when available;
- volumetrics and graded exposure.

Avoid 4K photographic wood/stone textures that make the set stop reading as Minecraft.

Do not bundle or redistribute official/proprietary textures unless properly licensed. When needed, create original Minecraft-inspired pixel materials.

## 3. Camera-driven environment design

Build what the planned cameras can see. Spend detail budget on silhouettes, foreground anchors, set dressing, and surfaces that appear in close shots.

For each camera, inspect:

- foreground occluders;
- subject silhouette;
- midground story elements;
- background shape/scale;
- practical light positions;
- exits/entrances needed by action.

A 60-second short rarely needs a massive explorable map.

## 4. Shot vocabulary

Useful camera roles:

- **Establishing:** ultra-wide or wide; define biome, scale, weather, and geography.
- **Character:** medium/wide; introduce posture and movement.
- **Over-shoulder:** reveal a target, threat, portal, structure, or destination.
- **Action:** lateral or three-quarter view that preserves silhouettes during interactions.
- **Detail/insert:** hero prop, hand interaction, block placement, crafting, mechanism.
- **Hero:** low-angle or composed frontal shot for commitment, victory, or confrontation.
- **Emotional:** closer static framing for a pause or relationship beat.
- **Ending:** wide pull-back, locked tableau, or slow reveal that leaves an afterimage.

Default to pre-rigged named cameras rather than one omnipresent flying camera.

## 5. Camera motion

Use movement only when it changes information or emotion.

Preferred moves:

- slow push-in to increase tension/attention;
- slow dolly-out to reveal isolation or consequence;
- truck/pan to follow action;
- tilt to reveal height/scale;
- subtle handheld only when danger or instability justifies it.

Avoid constant orbit, high-speed drone motion, purposeless zooms, or spinning transitions.

## 6. Lighting as story

Practical game-world lights should motivate the cinematic light whenever possible:

- lantern/torch -> warm local pool;
- lava -> orange/red up-light and bounce;
- Nether portal -> purple/magenta source;
- beacon -> cool white/blue column;
- redstone -> small red accents;
- moon/snow -> cool ambient fill;
- fire -> warm flicker with moving shadows.

When a prop turns on/off, the environment must respond. A glowing lantern that does not illuminate nearby blocks is a QC failure.

## 7. Color design

Choose one dominant contrast connected to the emotional arc:

- cold blue vs warm orange -> danger/solitude vs shelter/belonging;
- violet vs black -> alien/End atmosphere;
- lava orange vs deep crimson -> Nether pressure;
- green-cyan vs near-black -> Deep Dark unease;
- pale dawn gold vs blue shadow -> renewal.

Let the story transition between the two sides rather than displaying every color equally.

## 8. Weather and atmosphere

Use weather as cinematic production design:

- snow -> survival, loneliness, comfort contrast;
- rain -> tension, tragedy, combat;
- fog -> horror, Ancient City, mystery;
- embers -> Nether, destruction, aftermath;
- dust/sand -> desert ruins, travel;
- spores/pollen -> enchanted grove, quiet wonder.

Keep particles spatially plausible. Do not allow outdoor snow/rain to fill sealed interiors unless the scene supports it.

## 9. Composition and scale

Create depth with at least three layers when possible:

1. foreground block/branch/terrain;
2. character/action plane;
3. architecture/landscape background.

Use negative space to make a lone character feel small. Use strong diagonals from block edges, bridges, mine shafts, roof lines, or cliff faces to guide the eye.

For scale reveals, keep the protagonist small in frame and give the world a recognizable block-size reference.

## 10. Continuity

Track across shots:

- character facing and screen direction;
- prop hand/attachment;
- block changes and damage;
- weather intensity;
- time-of-day direction;
- camera side of action;
- practical light state;
- companion/threat positions.

If a block is placed, broken, lit, or moved, every later shot must inherit the change unless a transition clearly explains otherwise.
